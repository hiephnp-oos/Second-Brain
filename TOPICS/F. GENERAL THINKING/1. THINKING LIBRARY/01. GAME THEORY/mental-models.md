# Game Theory — Mental Models

Source: *Thinking Strategically* — Dixit & Nalebuff (GT-01)

These are operational reasoning models distilled from the source. They are not universal laws; they are prompts for analysis.

## GT-MM-01 — Model the interaction, not just your decision

Before choosing an action, identify:

1. Who are the relevant players?
2. What does each player want?
3. What actions are available to each?
4. What does each player know?
5. How will each player respond to my action?
6. How does that response change my outcome?

Use when another actor can materially change the result.

## GT-MM-02 — Look forward, then reason backward

For sequential situations:

1. identify the later decision;
2. determine what the later actor would rationally prefer at that point;
3. propagate that response backward;
4. choose today's action using the resulting future response.

Do not evaluate your current move as if later actors were passive.

## GT-MM-03 — Simplify before solving

Use this order:

1. remove dominated strategies;
2. identify dominant strategies;
3. map best responses;
4. look for equilibrium;
5. only then use more elaborate reasoning.

This reduces unnecessary complexity.

## GT-MM-04 — Stability is not desirability

Ask two separate questions:

- What outcome is strategically stable?
- Is that outcome actually desirable?

An equilibrium can be stable while being worse for all players than another feasible outcome.

## GT-MM-05 — Change the game, not just the argument

If an actor will not respond as desired under the current rules, ask:

> Can I change the incentives, timing, information, options, or response rules?

Strategic improvement can come from changing the game structure rather than persuading the player to behave differently inside the old structure.

## GT-MM-06 — A threat has value only if it is credible

For any threat or promise, ask:

1. What action am I committing to?
2. After the other side moves, would I still want to carry it out?
3. If not, what makes the commitment credible?
4. Does the commitment change the other side's expected response?

If the answer to #3 is weak, treat the statement as information or bluff rather than a reliable strategic commitment.

## GT-MM-07 — Commitment can create strategic value by removing options

Do not assume more freedom is always better.

Sometimes deliberately reducing your own future options:
- changes the opponent's expectations;
- prevents your own reversal;
- makes a threat or promise credible;
- changes the sequence of decisions.

The value comes from the response induced in the other actor.

## GT-MM-08 — Detectability and punishment determine cooperation

When cooperation is vulnerable to cheating, ask:

- Can cheating be observed?
- Can the true cause of the observed outcome be distinguished from noise?
- Can the cheater be identified?
- Is punishment credible?
- Is punishment proportionate enough to avoid unnecessary retaliation?
- Is the future valuable enough to support cooperation?

If observation is poor, an enforcement mechanism may punish innocent behavior or fail to punish real defection.

## GT-MM-09 — Forgiveness is a strategic parameter under noisy observation

In repeated interaction with possible misperception:

- punish every apparent defection → retaliation can echo indefinitely;
- forgive everything → exploitation becomes easier;
- use evidence over multiple time horizons → distinguish isolated error from persistent opportunism.

The appropriate forgiveness level depends on error probability and the relative value of future versus current outcomes.

## GT-MM-10 — Randomize when predictability is exploitable

Ask:

> Can the other side improve its outcome by predicting my next action?

If yes, an intentionally mixed strategy may prevent exploitation.

Randomization is useful only when the proportions and strategic context justify it.

## GT-MM-11 — Separate randomization from brinkmanship

Randomization:
- chooses among actions unpredictably.

Brinkmanship:
- creates a risk that an undesirable outcome may occur because control is deliberately reduced.

Do not use "unpredictable" as a blanket explanation for both.

## GT-MM-12 — Bargaining is a game with rules

Before negotiating, model:

- who moves first;
- who can reject;
- what happens after rejection;
- how delay changes the value of the deal;
- whether the sequence itself creates leverage.

Changing procedure can change the outcome even when the underlying interests are unchanged.

## GT-MM-13 — Ask what incentive the rule creates

For any process, policy, contract, or workflow:

> If every actor optimizes for their own objective under these rules, what behavior will the rules produce?

Then test whether that behavior is the one the system actually needs.

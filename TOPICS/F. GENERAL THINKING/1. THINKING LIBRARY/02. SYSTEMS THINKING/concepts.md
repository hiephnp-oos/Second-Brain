# Systems Thinking — Concepts

Source: Book 06 *Thinking in Systems* — Donella H. Meadows.

## Core structure
A system is a set of elements, interconnections and a purpose whose structure generates characteristic behavior. Events are outputs of deeper structure, not always isolated causes. The same elements can behave differently when their relationships, rules or purpose change.

## Stocks and flows
Stocks accumulate or deplete over time. Flows change stocks. The state of a stock depends on the history of inflows and outflows, creating inertia and delays between intervention and visible effect. A flow can change quickly while the stock it affects changes gradually.

## Feedback
A feedback loop closes a causal chain: the state of a stock affects decisions or physical processes that then change the stock. Reinforcing feedback amplifies change; balancing feedback counteracts change relative to a goal or constraint. Multiple loops can interact, and their relative strengths can shift over time.

## Bathtub dynamics and dynamic equilibrium
A bathtub is a simple stock-and-flow model: water level is the stock, faucet inflow and drain outflow are flows. Equal inflow and outflow hold the stock steady even while material continues moving. A stock's level does not instantly match a change in flow; accumulated quantity and flow rates must be distinguished.

## One-stock system patterns
A single stock can be controlled by competing balancing loops with different goals, as in a thermostat balancing room temperature against both a desired setting and heat exchange with the environment. A single stock can also be driven by reinforcing growth while balancing constraints or depletion increasingly oppose it. The resulting behavior depends on loop structure, relative strength, limits and delays—not on the stock count alone.

## Two-stock system patterns
Two stocks can be coupled when a flow or feedback relationship links their rates of change. Population and productive capital, inventory and replenishment, or a resource and the capacity exploiting it can produce behavior that cannot be understood by analyzing either stock in isolation. Delays in information or response can cause overshoot and oscillation; reinforcing growth may dominate initially and then be constrained by balancing feedback or resource limits.

## Resilience, self-organization and hierarchy
Resilience is a system's capacity to persist and recover under variable conditions. It depends on a rich set of feedbacks and response options, not merely on keeping the system's visible state constant. A system can appear stable while losing the capacity to absorb disturbance; optimizing short-term efficiency or uniformity can reduce resilience.

Self-organization is a system's capacity to generate or change its own structure. Local interactions, simple organizing rules, diversity, experimentation and room to adapt can produce new capabilities. Excessive control may suppress this capacity, while unbounded freedom is not itself a guarantee of good outcomes.

Hierarchy organizes systems into subsystems and larger systems. This can reduce complexity and support resilience when each level has appropriate autonomy and useful information links. Hierarchies fail when a subsystem optimizes its own goal at the expense of the larger system, or when the higher level forgets that its role is to serve the functioning of its subsystems and whole.

## Surprises: behavior, nonlinearity, boundaries, limits and delays
System events are snapshots; behavior is the pattern over time; structure is the interacting stocks, flows and feedback that generates the pattern. Event-only explanations tend to treat recurring outcomes as isolated incidents. Better diagnosis moves between observed events, long-term behavior and the structure that could produce it.

Nonlinear relationships mean that causes and effects are not proportional across all conditions. Small changes can have large effects, and the dominant feedback loop can change as the system moves into a different operating region. Linear extrapolation is unsafe when relationships, constraints or the state of the system change.

System boundaries are modeling choices, not always natural or fixed edges. Excluding a source, sink, actor, cost, feedback link or long-term consequence can make a model misleading and can merely transfer a problem elsewhere. Expand or contract the boundary according to the question, but make the choice explicit.

Limits often interact rather than acting as one fixed ceiling. Growth can change the system and its constraints, expose new bottlenecks or shift which limit dominates. A useful model tests several plausible limits and how they change over time instead of assuming one constant carrying capacity.

Delays separate an action from its consequences and can make feedback arrive too late. Delayed balancing feedback can cause overshoot or oscillation; delayed information can also lead decision-makers to overcorrect or misread the current state. Map the delay between action, observation and effective response.

Bounded rationality means actors make decisions from limited information, attention, time and mental models. Their responses may be locally reasonable from their own perspective yet create an unintended aggregate result. Explain what each actor can see and what incentives or goals shape their response instead of assuming a single omniscient decision-maker.

## System traps and opportunities
System traps are recurring structures that generate undesirable behavior. Identify the feedback mechanism before selecting a fix; the matching intervention is part of the diagnosis.

- Policy resistance: actors pull a shared stock toward different goals, so each intervention triggers counter-responses. The opportunity is to involve affected actors and find aligned or higher-level goals rather than simply overpowering resistance.
- Tragedy of the commons: users receive direct benefits from shared-resource use while spreading the costs of depletion, weakening feedback from resource condition to individual decisions. Restore that feedback through shared rules, access limits or another governance arrangement suited to the resource.
- Drift to low performance: standards are adjusted downward to match deteriorating results, reinforcing the decline. Keep standards anchored to an explicit reference and use demonstrated better performance to improve them.
- Escalation: each actor responds by surpassing the other, creating a reinforcing race toward extremes. Avoid entering the loop; if caught in it, interrupt competition or negotiate balancing constraints.
- Success to the successful: early winners gain more resources or opportunities to win again, concentrating outcomes and excluding alternatives. Consider diversification, limits on concentration or rewards that do not automatically bias the next round.
- Shifting the burden to the intervenor: symptom relief substitutes for solving the underlying problem and can erode the system's own capacity, increasing dependence on the intervention. Restore internal capability and focus on structural causes.
- Rule beating: actors appear to comply with a rule while gaming it in ways that distort the system. Treat this behavior as feedback about the rule's design, incentives or purpose and redesign accordingly.
- Seeking the wrong goal: a system efficiently pursues indicators that incompletely represent its real purpose. Define goals and measures around actual system welfare and outcomes, not proxies such as effort or easily counted output.

## Information, rules and goals
Information flows, rules, constraints and goals are structural variables. Decision-makers cannot respond accurately to information they do not receive, receive too late, or interpret incorrectly. A metric can alter behavior by rewarding a proxy rather than the intended result. Changing these structures can alter behavior without changing the physical elements.

## Leverage and practical synthesis
Leverage can lie in information flows, feedback structures, rules, goals and deeper assumptions. The hierarchy of leverage is a heuristic, not a guarantee; practical leverage must be assessed alongside feasibility, delays, resistance and side effects.

Observe behavior over time → identify stocks and flows → map reinforcing and balancing feedback → locate nonlinearities, delays and interacting limits → audit the system boundary and actor perspectives → diagnose any recurring system trap → inspect information, rules and goals → test structural leverage and unintended consequences.

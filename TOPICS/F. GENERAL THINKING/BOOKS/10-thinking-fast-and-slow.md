# 10 — Thinking, Fast and Slow

**Source:** Daniel Kahneman, *Thinking, Fast and Slow*  
**Review status:** Core cognitive-bias source

## Core thesis
Human judgment relies heavily on fast, associative processes and can be improved when slower, more effortful reasoning is engaged where intuition is unreliable.

## Reusable knowledge
- System 1 is fast, associative, and low-effort; System 2 is slower and effortful. The distinction is a useful model, not a literal two-part brain architecture.
- Anchoring can pull estimates toward irrelevant reference values.
- Availability makes memorable or salient events feel more probable.
- Representativeness can cause base-rate neglect.
- Framing changes choices even when underlying outcomes are equivalent.
- Loss aversion means losses often carry more psychological weight than comparable gains.
- Regression to the mean can be mistaken for causal improvement or deterioration.
- Narrow framing can create poor decisions when options should be evaluated as a portfolio.
- Confidence and narrative coherence can exceed evidential support.
- Outside-view methods and simple formulas can outperform unaided intuition in appropriate domains.

## Application
Before consequential judgment ask:
`What is the reference class? What is the base rate? What is the anchor? What would regression to the mean predict? What changes under a different frame?`

## Limitation
Some specific psychological findings discussed in the book have been debated or revised in later research. Use the mechanisms as hypotheses and decision prompts, not as infallible empirical laws.

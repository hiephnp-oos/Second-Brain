# 01 — Thinking Strategically

**Source:** Avinash K. Dixit & Barry J. Nalebuff, *Thinking Strategically*  
**Review status:** Core operational source

## Core thesis
Many decisions are interdependent: the best action depends on what other actors will do, and their actions depend on what they expect you to do. Strategic reasoning therefore requires modeling the interaction rather than optimizing in isolation.

## Reusable knowledge
- Identify players, actions, information, incentives, and possible outcomes.
- Distinguish a decision from the response it induces.
- Work backward from likely responses when choices are sequential.
- Look for dominant strategies, but do not assume dominance exists.
- In simultaneous settings, examine best responses and equilibrium rather than simply choosing the locally attractive move.
- Randomization can be rational when predictability would be exploitable.
- Credible commitments change behavior only when the other side believes the commitment will actually be carried out.
- Threats and promises are strategic instruments only when incentives support them.
- In repeated interaction, reputation and future payoffs can change today's optimal action.
- Strategic moves can work by changing the game itself: information, timing, options, commitments, or incentives.

## Application
Before an important decision ask:
1. Who are the relevant actors?
2. What does each actor want?
3. What does each actor know?
4. What do they believe I will do?
5. What do I believe they will do?
6. Which actions are credible?
7. What changes if the interaction repeats?

## Limitation
The framework is strongest when players, incentives, and available actions can be specified reasonably well. Real organizations often contain ambiguous preferences, bounded rationality, coalitions, and incomplete information.
